*A post after the series [CQRS with effect-cqrs](../), on its lending library. November 2026.*

Sagas are the part of CQRS that teams skip most often. Aggregates and read models make
sense on day one, while a saga looks like ceremony: a workflow, a trigger, an engine, just to
lend a book? It is so much shorter to let the member's command do everything at once.

This post takes that shortcut, twice, on the library of the series. Both versions compile,
pass a happy-path test, and look fine in a demo. Then we look at what they do to the data,
and at what the saga of [part 4](../sagas/) does differently. Every snippet runs in
[`examples/blog/without-saga.test.ts`](https://github.com/davidnussio/effect-cqrs/blob/main/examples/blog/without-saga.test.ts).

The rules are the ones from the series: **a member may have at most three books at a time,
and a copy can only be with one member.** The first rule is about the member, the second
about the book.

## Mistake 1: one aggregate records everything

Borrowing changes the member and the book, so the member's command records both changes.

It reads well. One command, one atomic append, both facts recorded together. Here is a
morning at the library:

<Code code={region(withoutSaga, "greedy-run")} lang="ts" title={file} />

Alice and Bob both have the only copy of *Dune*. Nothing failed, so nothing told us.

Look at where the rule "one member per copy" is checked in `borrow`: nowhere, and it can't
be. The handler decides from the *member's* state, which holds the member's loans and
nothing about the book. The member wrote a fact about the book, `BookLent`, without asking
the book.

And the event is in the wrong place. `BookLent` sits in Alice's stream and in Bob's, not in
`book-1`'s, so the book's own state says it is on the shelf. Every read model that follows
the book's events agrees with it. The library now has two versions of the truth, and the
one in the book's stream is wrong.

The usual patch is to read the book first, with `books.readModel(bookId)`, and refuse if it
is lent. That only moves the problem: the book's stream still never gets the `BookLent`, so
the read always says available. Even if it did, reading and then writing elsewhere is
check-then-act: between the read and the member's append, another member can borrow the
same copy, and no version check stops it, because the append goes to a different stream.

<Aside>
An aggregate is a consistency boundary. It can guarantee its rules because every change to
its data goes through its own commands, in turn, checked against its own version. **A rule
can only be enforced by the aggregate that owns the data the rule is about.**
</Aside>

This version passes every test you'd think of writing first. The problem is in a test you
wouldn't: one where something goes wrong *between* the two writes.

`borrow` now writes to two streams: the book's, inside `lend`, then the member's, when the
handler returns. Those are two appends. No transaction spans them, and in general none can:
the two streams may live in different tables, databases or services. So let's lose the
connection to the database at the worst moment, with a store that fails one append:

<Code code={region(withoutSaga, "failing-once")} lang="ts" title={file} />

Same code, same request, on that store:

<Code code={region(withoutSaga, "calling-failure")} lang="ts" title={file} />

The book is lent to Carol, for loan `loan-3`. Carol has no loan `loan-3`. The command
failed, so the client tells Carol to try again later, and if she doesn't, the book stays
lent to a loan that doesn't exist. Nothing in the system knows that a loan was half done,
so nothing will come back to finish it or undo it.

A lost connection is only one way to get there. Any of these does the same:

- **The process stops** between the two appends: a crash, a deploy, a scaled-down pod.
- **The member's append loses a race.** Another request for Carol got there first, so the
  service reloads her state and runs the handler again, which calls `lend` again. Here
  `lend` happens to be idempotent, so the second call records nothing. If the handler had
  instead picked "the first free copy", the retry could lend a second one.
- **The member's rules change their mind on the retry.** After the reload Carol has three
  books, so the retry fails with `LoanLimitReached`, after the book was lent.

There are quieter costs too. A handler is meant to only decide, because it can run more
than once. The member module now depends on the books service, and an aggregate whose
handler calls another aggregate's command can deadlock if that one ever calls back, since
commands on one aggregate take turns. And when someone asks why a book is lent, the
member's stream has no answer.

## The same loan, with a saga

The saga starts from a different question. Not "how do I write both changes at once?", which
has no good answer, but "how do I make sure the process *finishes*?"

The member only ever records its own decision. `requestLoan` checks the member's rule and
appends `LoanRequested` to the member's stream, one atomic write to one aggregate. From that
moment, the intention to borrow is a fact in the log, and the rest of the process can always
be finished from it.

Look at what it does with the data. It reads the member id, the book id and the loan id from
the member's event, and hands them to the *book's* command. The book decides with its own
state and its own rule. The answer goes back to the *member*, as one of the member's own
commands, `confirmLoan` or `cancelLoan`. Every change is still made by a command on the
aggregate that owns the data. The saga owns no data, and changes none directly: it puts
together what several aggregates know, and asks each one to do its part.

The loan settles: the book is lent to Carol, and Carol has the loan. Here is why:

- **Each step is one command on one aggregate**, with one atomic append. A step either
  happened or it didn't. There is no half of a step.
- **A step that fails is tried again.** `Saga.command` retries a lost race or a store error a
  few times, with a backoff that starts at 50 milliseconds, so a dropped connection costs a
  short wait.
- **Finished steps are not done again.** The workflow engine records the outcome of every
  step. A run that restarts after a crash skips the book's `lend`, which already happened,
  and goes on with the confirmation. With a durable engine, that holds across restarts of
  the whole process.
- **The commands are idempotent**, so a step that ran but whose outcome wasn't recorded can
  run again harmlessly.
- **The state in between is explicit.** Until the saga confirms or cancels it, Carol's loan
  is `requested`. It holds one of her three places, so she can't over-borrow while the book
  decides, and it shows that something is in progress. If a failure outlasts the retries,
  the run stops there, and the loan stays visibly `requested` instead of silently missing.

Here are the three versions side by side:

| | One aggregate records both | One aggregate calls the other | Saga |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The book's rule is checked | No | Yes | Yes |
| The book's events are in its stream | No | Yes | Yes |
| A failure between two writes | — | Leaves them disagreeing | Retried, then resumed |
| Where the process is recorded | Nowhere | Nowhere | The member's events and the engine |

## Rules of thumb

- **One command, one aggregate, one append.** When a command needs to change a second
  aggregate, the first one records what it decided, and something else, a saga, asks the
  second.
- **An event names its own aggregate's facts.** A member's event that says "the book was
  lent" is a fact the member had no right to decide.
- **A decision that needs another aggregate's state is a process, not a command.** Model the
  steps in between, such as `requested`, in the state, because they are real: the book really
  hasn't answered yet.
- **If you can't live with the step in between, check the boundary.** A rule that must hold
  instantly across two aggregates may mean they are one aggregate. That is a design
  decision, and sometimes the right one. But "every loan in the library" is too big to be one
  aggregate, so here the saga wins.

What about a view model whose handler runs the book's command? It gets closer: the member
records its intention, and the handler sees every event at least once. But it keeps no
record of which steps ran or which branch it took, so it can't resume a half-done process or
replay one the same way. The saga's trigger *is* a view model, plus the workflow engine that
keeps that record.
